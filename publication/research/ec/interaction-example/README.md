# A negative one-at-a-time effect and a positive joint effect

Fix a group L of applicants whose visible lists never change. A disjoint set H receives changed recommendations. Write F(S) for the number of matched applicants in L when exactly S ⊆ H receive their new lists, with applicant-proposing deferred acceptance rerun from scratch. All underlying rankings, acceptable pairs, and list lengths stay fixed. The sum of single-person effects is ∑ᵢ[F({i})−F(∅)]. The joint effect is F(H)−F(∅). The interaction residual J is the joint effect less that sum.

**Construction.** There are three listings x, y, z. Three lower-group applicants each see only their respective listing. The first two higher-group applicants see x initially and the third sees y. Each would switch its recommendation to z. Any common strict priority putting all three higher-group applicants ahead of all lower-group applicants suffices; every listing uses that order restricted to its eligible applicants. Each higher applicant ranks its own original listing above z and both above being unmatched. Its score preferences and those of other participants are identical under all runs. Each visible list contains one listing.

| Applicants receiving the new list | L applicants matched | Change |
|---|---|---:|
| Nobody | z | 0 |
| First higher applicant alone | nobody | −1 |
| Second higher applicant alone | nobody | −1 |
| Third higher applicant alone | y | 0 |
| All three | x and y | +1 |

One switch opens no room at x because another higher-priority renter still takes it. Sending several higher applicants to z consumes only one new unit of capacity. The singleton effects sum to −2 even as the joint effect is +1. A group expected to lose placements under one-at-a-time extrapolation therefore gains under the joint policy in this finite market.

**Sharp bounds at quota one.** Let h≥2 higher-group applicants change a single recommendation. For any number of listings and stationary applicants with arbitrary strict listing priorities, the count residual satisfies −h ≤ J ≤ h−1+⌊h/2⌋. Both bounds are tight.

To verify the formula, collapse stationary applicants at each listing to its highest-priority stationary applicant. The listing can contribute to L only if this stationary winner belongs to L. An initial (final) threat is a switching applicant who targets the listing before (after) and outranks that stationary winner. Ignore listings with a persistent threat, since their contribution to L is identically zero. Write d and a for the numbers of removed and added threats, which are now disjoint. The listing-level residual is:

| Removed threats d | Added threats a | Contribution to J |
|---|---|---:|
| 0 | a ≥ 1 | a−1 |
| d ≥ 2 | 0 | +1 |
| 1 | a ≥ 1 | −1 |
| All remaining cases | | 0 |

For the first row, each singleton causes a loss but the joint switch loses the listing only once. For the second, all original threats have to depart to release the listing. For the third, a singleton departure frees the listing, but an arriving threat under joint treatment cancels that gain. The other cases yield zero by the same occupancy check. There are at most h additions and h removals, so the total of the first row is at most h−1 and there are at most ⌊h/2⌋ cases in the second row. There are at most h negative cases. This proves both inequalities. For the upper extremum put two applicants at each of ⌊h/2⌋ original occupied listings (one extra singleton origin if h is odd), and send all h to the same fresh lower-occupied listing. For the lower extremum put one higher applicant at each of h listings, each with its own stationary L applicant, and rotate the higher group by one listing. Every singleton frees its origin, yet the joint rotation frees none. All these witnesses admit a common strict priority with the higher group first. This is an extremal result for a one-slot reranking. Padding below gives larger-quota lower-bound constructions without purporting to give universal upper bounds for larger quotas.

**What the rollout curve measures.** If each of the h higher applicants independently receives the new recommendation with probability p, a stationary lower winner with no persistent threat receives listing s with probability pᵈ(1−p)ᵃ. Every departed threat must switch and no newly arriving threat can switch. Taking the sum over these listings gives the expected number of lower matches R(p). Alternatively, when j of h switchers are selected uniformly without replacement, the same probability is C(h−d−a,j−d)/C(h,j), treating combinatorially impossible choices as zero. For any set function F and independent adoption, R′(0) is the sum of singleton effects by differentiation of the subset mixture. Hence J=R(1)−R(0)−R′(0). In the paired-origin family, R(p) = m p² + (1−p)^(2m): the initial slope is −2m while the full treatment gains m−1 placements. This formula is specific to the model, and the derivative equality holds for any binary policy-switch experiment.

**Minimum number of people switching at quota one.** At least three higher applicants must change lists for a strictly negative sum of singleton effects and a strictly positive joint effect. This holds with arbitrary strict priorities and arbitrary stationary applicants, including higher applicants. To see it, collapse the stationary applicants at each listing to the highest-priority one. It contributes one L match if and only if that stationary winner is in L and none of the higher switchers currently targeting that listing outranks the winner. With two switchers, write eligible initial/final “threats” at listing s for the set of those two who target s in that state and outrank its stationary lower-group winner. A listing's contribution to J is zero unless both switches flip their threat status there. Flips in opposite directions yield −1. Two removals or two additions yield +1. Strict signs require the integer J to be at least two, hence at least two positive terms. Two removals can occur only at a shared original listing and two additions only at a shared final listing. Each can occur at most once. Having both requires two threatening applicants to move from the same original listing to the same final listing. Their joint move frees exactly one L placement and displaces exactly one L placement, giving joint change zero. This contradicts the strict positive joint effect. One switch has exactly its own singleton effect. The example with three switches therefore attains the minimum under the quota-one assumptions.

**Every singleton can be harmful, with a growing joint gain.** Take m≥2 listings x₁,…,xₘ plus one listing z, with one L applicant restricted to each listing. Place two higher applicants at each xᵢ initially. Each higher applicant would switch to z. For a coalition S, the level F(S) equals the number of xᵢ for which S contains both original higher applicants, plus the indicator that S is empty. Thus each of 2m singleton changes is −1. The joint effect is m−1; the interaction residual is 3m−1. In this family the feasible graph is connected because each original xᵢ is connected to z through its higher applicants. These are count effects. On a percentage-of-L basis divide by m+1 and multiply by 100; the cited count residual's linear growth is not a claim of an unbounded rate for an individual policy.

**Equal quotas larger than one.** For any k≥2 add k dummy listings and k auxiliary applicants. Each auxiliary sees all k dummies and ranks its dedicated dummy first. Put all auxiliaries ahead of the core population in the common priority. Core applicants retain their designated core choice and are also shown the same k−1 dummies. They rank those below their core choices. The disjoint dedicated first choices are held by auxiliaries throughout DA, so the core outcomes are unchanged. Every applicant has exactly k distinct recommendations; each changing applicant replaces precisely one recommendation.

verify.py replays every subset for the three-switch instance and three larger family sizes, at quotas one and five, and checks the closed form. The sign and extremal families are additionally checked via explicit profiles up to 10 switchers, and 6,400 randomly generated arbitrary threat profiles compare the exact formula against direct subset outcomes. A separate exhaustive local enumeration checks the two-switch impossibility in 90,000 configurations, allowing listing-specific threat statuses. Those configurations exhaust stationarity after suppressing unchanged listings, but the proof above is sufficient without enumeration. A one-command replay writes the deterministic evidence.

~~~sh
python3 -B verify.py --out replay.json
~~~

This example provides a mechanism and a limit to one-at-a-time projection. It does not identify the cause of a particular empirical residual. In particular some lower-group recipients can gain or lose through rejection chains even when joint lower-group counts coincide.
