# Referee assessment: More First Choices, Fewer Placements

This assessment concerns the preceding revision at commit 9660fdc. The [subsequent evidence](behavior/README.md) links actual enrollment and tests the assumed response to recommendations.

**My vote on the incoming draft: weak reject. On the revision: borderline / weak accept as an empirical market-design paper.** This is a simulated review, not a prediction of the program committee. I would defend the revised paper for the evidence about who bears the costs of curation and the exact limits of protecting old contracts. I would still oppose presenting it as a field evaluation or as a general impossibility theorem.

**The objection that changed the paper.** The earlier headline could leave a reader thinking that accurate recommendations reduce welfare or that the two fitted predictors lie on a frontier. Neither follows. Relative to the rank-blind rule, adding rank produced 2,218 more family-entered first choices and 1,787 fewer placements over four Chilean cohorts. On original household preferences in NYC, the two curves cross there, too. Among Chilean records with edited lists, 8,076 moved up and 2,200 down. For unchanged lists the corresponding counts were 2,254 up and 7,215 down. These are paired outcomes in replay; the list-change groups are selected. The [rank evidence](audit/chile/README.md) distinguishes reported choices from administrative fallbacks and keeps everyone in the denominator.

**The comparator I would have requested.** If a clearinghouse knows the reports and rules, why fit a separate logistic prediction for each edge? We implemented a predictor that estimates full-list partner frequencies by clearing the market with simulated tie orders. When evaluated at the withheld published order in 2022 and 2023, the 64-draw rule showed 494–541 more recorded offers and placed 25–56 more pupils than rank-informed, across eight batches per cohort. It also gave 106–163 fewer first choices. It still placed fewer than rank-blind, and at 16 draws it improved both endpoints against rank-informed in only five of 16 cases. Using simulated DA for admission prediction is itself established in [Arteaga et al., online appendix](https://www.christopher-neilson.com/work/documents/Warnings/OnlineAppendix_AKNZ_June2021.pdf). The comparison is a counterexample to a frontier interpretation. It is not evidence that one should deploy this rule or that Monte Carlo learning always helps. In a follow-up we fixed the rule before computing its previously unseen outcomes for 2024, a cohort already used for other analyses. All eight 64-draw batches gained 447–483 historical hits and 2–24 placements and lost 105–123 first choices. No 16-draw batch gained a placement; three of eight did at 32. We excluded 2025 because its official tie rule changed. The stronger full-information control, which is allowed to see the realized priority order, beats rank-informed on coverage and placement in all four years and is identified separately.

**What is left of the theory?** The baseline stability check is elementary, with a close one-to-one antecedent in [Boehmer et al., Observation 1](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.12755). The contribution I would defend is the omission/turnover bound with a distinct displaced witness, the full characterization of permitted fixed-quota edits as applied here, and the empirical diagnosis it enables. A row-by-row score sort is not a difficult algorithm. Its exactness makes a numerical finding interpretable: even with every feasible slot available and the true reference supplied, 9,333 of the 9,407 full-list partners missed by the Chilean baseline cannot be shown without blocking that baseline allocation. The 74 remaining targets can all be recovered. This is not a limit on all policies that avoid a net loss of placements. The stable assignment must remain **supportable**; DA may improve some recipients' assignments. The score-based design improved 12 school assignments while retaining every recipient. Proofs establish the claims; independently implemented and exhaustive finite checks guard against mismatches between the proofs and the programs. [Evidence and check instructions](audit/slots/SLOT_METHODS.md).

**Do the two markets belong together?** Yes, if the object is a binding shortlist under finite capacity. NYC is where we can ask whether any affordable alternative exists; we cannot observe a household's rank order, when all units were simultaneously on the market, or who signed a lease. Chile supplies a distinct challenge: submitted ranks and historical offers, a real capacity constraint, and a protected continuity option that defeats a pure-prefix guarantee. The same first-five rule fixes a common benchmark. Individual-slot protection gives the comparison a second purpose: under the specified rental rankings about one-third of missed partners can be safely added; under the school replay less than one percent can. Site-specific policies, reference targets and institutions differ; there is no pooled treatment effect and no evidence here about intermediary commissions.

**How much of the result survives empirical attacks?** The common rental result persists in 8,400 additional supported simulated markets using separate donors and listings, including all 39 supported design–borough–month cells. The fall enriched arm covers only 79.97% of its intended geographic-month weight, and the hosted rental model was not rerun on these data. The Chile signs survive an alternative larger seat convention both with menus held fixed and after recomputing score features. This convention reproduces historical assignments less well. Even the main replay misses 3.4–4.6% of official outcomes, while the placement effect is about one per thousand. No number of replications of that replay resolves behavior or mechanism misspecification. [NYC support ledger](audit/nyc-temporal/README.md) · [Chile capacity and replay checks](audit/chile/README.md).

**Originality is narrower than the title might suggest.** [Correa et al., Table 6](https://www.dii.uchile.cl/~jcorrea/papers/Journals/2022CEER.pdf) already produced more first choices and more nonassignment by changing Chilean lotteries. [Aguiar and Hong](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09693) distinguish feasibility from stability under limited attention, and [Munro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07217) studies spillovers in Chilean school choice. [Mukhopadhyay et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11514) compare welfare measures under changes to school assignment rules. The narrower question here is what happens when **the curation rule changes but priorities and the quota stay fixed**, including people whose menus never change, and how much of the predicted gain is compatible with a specified promise to incumbents. For real field evidence on how families respond to advice, see [Arteaga et al.](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjac013); this paper does not produce that evidence.

**What would change my remaining reservation?** Institutional validation rather than another parameter sweep. Work with an admissions authority to reproduce its full priority, reserve and family rules, inspect the disagreement cases, and re-estimate the key paired comparisons under that implementation. To claim an effect of *advice*, observe whether people actually follow it, including off-list applications and declined offers, with a design that respects competition between families. An analogous NYC claim would require linked listings, applications and lease outcomes. The current manuscript makes none of these claims. The named PDF is for team and course review; it has not been submitted. The [EC 2026 call](https://ec26.sigecom.org/call-for-contributions-acm/papers/) informs the review rubric, not future-cycle requirements.
