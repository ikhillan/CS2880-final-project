SLIDE 1 — Who gets a place?
00:00–00:18 | 18 seconds

We study how AI recommendations change access to opportunities with limited
capacity. A useful list for one person can change someone else's outcome.
Our question is simple: when everyone follows the recommendations, who gets a place?


SLIDE 2 — A better suggestion can hurt someone else
00:18–01:11 | 53 seconds

Start with two applicants and two school seats. Applicant A prefers school
x to school y. Applicant B accepts only x. School x gives A priority over B.

Initially, A is shown y and B is shown x. The blue lines show their assignments.
Both get a seat.

Now change only A's recommendation. A sees x instead of y. Both applicants
still see exactly one school. But they now compete for x, and A has higher
priority. A gets x, B gets no seat, and y stays empty.

The new suggestion helps A. B's own list has not changed at all, but B loses
access. That is the basic reason to evaluate recommendations jointly.
A person's outcome depends on what other people are encouraged to pursue.
This can happen in housing and jobs as well as schools.


SLIDE 3 — Choose a shortlist, then assign the places
01:11–02:06 | 55 seconds

We separate the recommendations from the assignment rule. First, the
recommender chooses a shortlist of feasible options for each person.
Then the assignment rule decides who gets which place.

We measure three things. A match means someone receives a place. Target
coverage means a particular reference choice appears on their shortlist.
A lost match means someone receives a place under one policy, but not under
the policy we compare it with.

The reference differs across our studies. In rentals it is the simulated
assignment when all feasible options are available. In Chile it is the
school recorded in later enrollment.

Our question is whether recommendations can improve while everyone who had
a place stays matched somewhere. They may switch schools. Keeping a seat
does not necessarily mean getting a better school.


SLIDE 4 — Same personal shortlists. Twice the match rate.
02:06–02:56 | 50 seconds

Here is our clearest finding. In the rental simulation, focus on renters
with only one to five feasible apartments. Every policy already shows them
all their options.

Their match rate rises from 13.1 percent to 27.5 percent when we change how
recommendations are coordinated across the market. That is more than double.
Yet their own shortlists stay exactly the same. Only other renters' shortlists
change.

We compare a policy coordinating one option with a policy balancing the
whole shortlist. I will explain both shortly. The result shows how much
competition can matter even when your own recommendations never change.
It is a simulation result, not measured housing outcomes.


SLIDE 5 — Why study rentals and school admissions?
02:56–04:07 | 71 seconds

The settings serve different purposes.

Rentals let us study actual saved GPT recommendations and compare ways to
coordinate them. Listings and household profiles come from market and survey
data, but renter preferences, landlord priorities, and resulting assignments
are modeled.

We reconstruct 1,280 markets and verify all 55,115 cached candidate hashes.
These new comparisons use previously analyzed calendar windows. Some homes
recur across them, so this is a follow-up analysis, not independent
property-level validation.

Chile gives us families' submitted rankings and the actual admissions rules.
Our reconstruction matches the released course or no-offer outcomes in about
two million audited applicant-years. That checks institutional fidelity.

The new shortlists still produce counterfactual outcomes under fixed reports.
The connection across settings is competition for limited capacity, not an
assumption that housing and schools behave identically.


SLIDE 6 — Two ways to coordinate recommendations
04:07–05:18 | 71 seconds

We test two simple coordination approaches.

The anchor first computes a maximum matching on all feasible pairs. It prefers
pairs already on the shortlists when several maximum matchings are available.
Each assigned option is then inserted into its recipient's shortlist, replacing
at most one option. List lengths stay fixed.

Balancing takes a broader approach. Count how many times each listing is
recommended. Minimize the sum of those counts squared, which discourages
everyone from seeing the same few listings. Among equally balanced shortlists,
favor options higher in the saved GPT ranking. We solve this exactly with
min-cost flow.

Both policies use feasible pairs and saved recommendations. Neither sees the
realized preference shocks, landlord priorities, or reference assignments.

Balancing can replace several options per person. It is a stronger coordination
control, not an equal-edit-budget comparison with the one-option anchor.
Neither approach imposes an individual retention constraint.


SLIDE 7 — Individual coverage misses the placement loss
05:18–06:13 | 55 seconds

First look at the original comparison with random shortlists of the same length.
Blue is reference-option coverage. Orange is placement. Zero means no
difference from random.

Across all three enriched calendar windows, GPT increases coverage by about
eight percentage points. Placement falls by about two to four points.
The directions repeat, and the original market-bootstrap intervals exclude zero.

This does not mean GPT makes worse predictions about everything. It means
that covering an individual's full-list assignment does not determine how
many people can be placed when everyone's shortlists interact.

These markets deliberately include affordable listings and renters with feasible
options. The ordinary sampling design and all uncertainty details are in the appendix.


SLIDE 8 — Coordination changes the comparison
06:13–07:20 | 67 seconds

Now put four policies on the same axes. Farther right means more reference
coverage. Higher means more people placed.

Random shortlists are on the left. GPT moves right, but slightly down.
The one-option anchor moves GPT upward, recovering placement with a small
change in coverage.

Balanced shortlists go much higher. Relative to the anchor, they gain another
12.6 percentage points of placement while losing about three points of
reference coverage in this panel.

The comparison matters. Relative to plain random, balancing improves both
outcomes. So the evidence does not show an unavoidable conflict between
coverage and placement.

These are point means for the September and October enriched panel. A control using extra information from the simulation improves both measures further. Detailed controls
and paired intervals are in the appendix. We have not established GPT as
the best recommender. This plot also cannot tell us whether any individual
is protected.


SLIDE 9 — A net gain does not tell us who gained
07:20–08:14 | 54 seconds

Return to the anchor and compare it with the original GPT shortlists on exactly
the same markets.

There are 449 newly placed renter appearances. There are also 37 exits:
people who were placed before and are now unplaced. Subtracting the exits
from the entrants gives the 412 net gain.

Reporting only that net gain would hide the loss of access. The more effective
balancing policy has the same issue. Compared with the anchor, it creates
many more placements but also causes 171 exits.

An exit is defined relative to a named policy. These counts describe repeated
simulated appearances, not unique households. Their role is to test an
individual protection claim that an average placement rate cannot establish.


SLIDE 10 — Why can an unchanged shortlist help you?
08:14–09:10 | 56 seconds

The cleanest competition result concerns renters with only one to five
feasible listings. Our policies show up to five options, so these renters
already see everything. Their personal shortlists cannot change.

When the whole market uses the anchor, this group's placement rate is 13.1
percent. Under balancing, it is 27.5 percent. That is a 14.4-point
gain even though the group's own recommendations are identical.

The change comes from other renters' shortlists. Coordinating those shortlists changes
who competes for each listing.

This is a sizable positive spillover inside the model. It still does not
protect every member of the group: 15 previously placed appearances lose
access under balancing. Better group outcomes and zero individual losses
remain different claims.


SLIDE 11 — Chile: the losses are concentrated
09:10–10:42 | 92 seconds

Rentals give us controlled comparisons, but their preferences are modeled.
Chile lets us examine the same access question using families' submitted
rankings and a reconstruction of the admissions rules.

We compare two classifiers trained on an earlier cohort to predict initial
offers. One uses submitted rank, and the other does not. They select short
shortlists for later cohorts. We score coverage against later observed enrollment
and replay admissions on the selected options.

The rank-informed policy gains 4,638 enrollment hits overall, but loses
1,670 placements relative to rank-blind shortlists.

The graph separates pupils by whether they historically received an initial
offer. Each bar uses its own group's denominator. Pupils with no initial
offer make up 7.4 percent of the linked sample but account for 76 percent
of the net placement loss.

That is 1,270 net lost placements in the smaller group. Its placement rate
falls by 0.873 percentage points in the counterfactual comparison.

These are not revoked official offers. Historical offer status defines the
groups; placement loss compares two new shortlist policies under fixed reported
preferences. The concentration of the loss is what motivates the protection
question.


SLIDE 12 — The comparison policy matters
10:42–11:37 | 55 seconds

There is an important check on that result. Compare the same rank-informed
policy with each family's first five discretionary courses instead.

Now it improves both aggregates: 9,800 more enrollment hits and 8,143 more
placements. Again, a prediction–placement conflict is not inevitable.

But the group without a historical initial offer still loses 2,860
counterfactual placements against this alternative.

That is why the comparison policy and the affected population both need
to be visible. An overall improvement can coexist with worse access for a
particular group. We should not treat the historical enrollment target as
welfare, or assume that a larger total resolves the distributional question.


SLIDE 13 — Choosing shortlists with zero exits is hard
11:37–12:38 | 61 seconds

Here is the mathematical design problem.

Fix the baseline shortlists and a supplied set of priority draws. Choose
one new shortlist policy that works across every draw. Maximize extra
reference hits, subject to every baseline recipient keeping a seat on
every draw. Recipients may switch schools, but cannot become unplaced.

In Chile, we start from rank-blind shortlists, preserve options shared with
rank-informed shortlists, fill from their union, and keep each quota.

Our theorem says that deciding whether a target gain is achievable is
NP-complete, even with unit seats and shortlists of length at most three.
Checking a chosen policy with deferred acceptance remains polynomial.

The theorem uses fixed, strict priorities on an explicit panel. Chile's
family-linked rules are tested separately. The difficulty is choosing
compatible edits, as the next slide illustrates.


SLIDE 14 — Why individually safe edits can conflict
12:38–13:34 | 56 seconds

The proof starts with a graph. Each vertex represents a beneficial
shortlist edit. Each edge creates one priority draw where the two endpoint
edits can conflict.

Look at this one draw. Selecting edit u displaces another, already-matched
pupil toward the fallback seat. Selecting edit v displaces a different
already-matched pupil toward the same seat. Either edit alone is safe. Select both, and one pupil must
lose a seat. Other vertex edits are harmless on this draw.

We create a draw for every edge. One fixed set of edits must work on all
of them. So a safe set cannot contain both endpoints of any edge.
That is an independent set.

Each selected vertex adds one reference hit. Maximizing safe gains therefore
solves the Independent Set problem. The appendix gives the full construction.


SLIDE 15 — Allowing school switches leaves more room
13:34–14:42 | 68 seconds

We then compare two protection requirements on the same Chilean design
panels: 1,024 lotteries per cohort for 2022 through 2024.

If every baseline recipient must keep the same course and seat type,
the maximum additional enrollment coverage is exactly 314 hits within our
allowed shortlist family.

If recipients may switch schools while keeping a seat, we construct shortlists
gaining 642 hits. The certified upper bound is 4,620. Allowing switches
therefore permits more than twice the demonstrated coverage gain.

But the bracket is wide. We have not found the optimal retention policy.

These are hindsight optimization benchmarks, using the evaluation cohorts'
later enrollment labels. They do not describe a policy that could choose its
shortlists before those outcomes existed. To make the constructive answer useful,
we also need a rule based only on information available before assignment.


SLIDE 16 — A test panel is weaker than a guarantee
14:42–15:33 | 51 seconds

We evaluate two rules frozen after applications arrive, before the ordinary
assignment. Neither uses that cohort's realized offers or later enrollment
to choose shortlists.

The structural rule protects a set of interactions around applicants without
a guaranteed fallback. Its proof covers all permitted ordinary lottery
orders under the fixed inputs and reconstructed mechanism.

It gains 28 enrollment hits and has zero exits in the test panels.

A learned rule passes its design-panel checks and gains 50 hits. But it
causes an exit in each of two fresh lottery generators.

The guaranteed gain is small. The useful distinction is between a proof
covering the allowed orders and a policy that merely passed many simulated
draws.


SLIDE 17 — Protecting recipients can preserve exclusion
15:33–16:36 | 63 seconds

The social objective cannot stop with protecting people who already have
a place. Our structural Chile rule produces no new entrants on the reported
paired test lotteries. It preserves incumbents, but does not expand access
for previously unplaced pupils in those tests.

That raises a substantive design choice. How should we balance protection
for existing recipients against opportunities for people who start excluded?

Our measurements are incomplete answers. Historical enrollment reflects
constraints as well as preference. Receiving any seat can mean moving to a
worse school, and modeled ranks are not measured household welfare.

Before deployment, people should retain the ability to add choices, correct
errors, and seek human review or appeal. Those are proposed safeguards,
not interventions we have tested. Recommendations redistribute existing
opportunities. They cannot create affordable apartments or new school
capacity.


SLIDE 18 — Evaluate the allocation, not just the list
16:36–17:06 | 30 seconds

Recommendations need a market-level evaluation.

Coordination can improve both coverage and placement. But better totals
do not guarantee that each person keeps access.

We measure that distinction, formalize the retention problem, and test
the gap between a finite test panel and a guarantee.

The next design problem is to improve access for people without a place
while protecting those who already have one. Thank you.
