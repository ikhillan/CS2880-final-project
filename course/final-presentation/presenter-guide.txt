SLIDE 1 — Who gets a place?
00:00–00:14 | 14 seconds

Imagine you and I both use an app to find an apartment. Yesterday you could get a place. Today the listings on your screen are identical, but the app has changed what it shows me. Could that cost you the apartment? That's the question we tested. Here's what happened.

SLIDE 2 — Same apartment lists. Twice the match rate.
00:14–00:55 | 41 seconds

In our New York rental simulation, some renters had one to five feasible apartments. The app showed them every one. We compared two ways of recommending apartments to the rest of the market.

With a rule that could replace at most one suggestion per renter, 13.1 percent of this group got an apartment.

With balanced shortlists across the market, 27.5 percent got one.

The rate more than doubled. The same people saw the same apartments, and each still faced the same landlord priorities. Only their competition changed. Here the group consists of 2,328 renter appearances in our September and October enriched markets. These are simulated assignments.

That sounds like a lot to attribute to somebody else's screen. A two-seat example shows how it can happen.

SLIDE 3 — A better suggestion can hurt someone else
00:55–01:35 | 40 seconds

Let's start with two applicants and two school seats. A prefers school x to school y. B accepts only x. And school x gives A priority over B.

At first, A sees y and B sees x. Both get a seat.

Now change only A's recommendation. Show A school x instead.

Both applicants still see just one option. But now they compete for the same seat. A gets x, B gets nothing, and y sits empty.

Imagine asking each person to review what the app showed them. A can say the suggestion improved. B can say the suggestion stayed the same. Put those answers together and you still haven't counted the lost seat. To see it, we have to run the assignment.

SLIDE 4 — How we measure a change in access
01:35–02:18 | 43 seconds

This example gives us a way to evaluate a recommender. First we ask whether the shortlist includes a reference option. We call that coverage. For rentals, the reference is the apartment a renter would be assigned if everyone saw every feasible listing. We measure rental coverage among people who get an apartment in that full-list assignment. In Chile we check whether a family's list includes the school where the child later enrolled.

Then we ask who gets a place. This time we run the assignment using everyone's shortlists together. Placement counts a person who gets any seat or apartment, even if it differs from the reference.

Finally, we pair the two runs person by person. Someone placed under the old policy and unplaced under the new one is an exit. For someone who gets a place for the first time, we count an entry. Two policies can place the same number of people and still produce both.

SLIDE 5 — Who would an audit of edited lists miss?
02:18–03:17 | 59 seconds

Housing and school admissions let us ask the same question with different evidence. For rentals we have actual listings and survey-based household profiles. Preferences, landlord priorities and assignments are modeled. We reconstructed 1,280 markets. Each comparison uses the same feasible apartments, renters and priority draws under both policies.

We use ordinary sampling and an enriched design with more affordable listings and more renters who have options. That distinction matters. Some markets could not be built under the Staten Island supply requirements. A household with no feasible apartment cannot gain access through a better ranking alone.

Chile gives us families' submitted rankings and an admissions mechanism we can replay. In the 2021 entry grades, only 6,133 out of roughly two hundred thousand linked pupils received a different list under the two policies. Yet 237 of 309 net lost placements fell among pupils with unchanged lists.

Most pupils were in that group to begin with. The point is what an audit would miss if it followed only people whose recommendations changed. These new assignments are counterfactual. The families supplied the rankings; our comparison changed the menus.

SLIDE 6 — Two ways to coordinate recommendations
03:17–04:42 | 85 seconds

The first rule coordinates one option. We call it the anchor. Find a maximum matching across all feasible pairs. When several maximum matchings exist, prefer pairs already on the shortlists. Then put each assigned option onto that person's list, replacing at most one option.

The second rule coordinates the whole shortlist. Count how often each listing is recommended, and minimize the sum of those counts squared. Concentrating recommendations on a few listings becomes expensive. Among equally balanced solutions, favor the saved GPT ranks. We solve this using standard min-cost flow.

The first recommendation to a listing costs one unit. The next costs three, then five. Those are the differences between successive squares. If two feasible apartments are equally useful for our tie breaker, the algorithm has a reason to recommend the less crowded one. Nobody has reserved it. Applicants still compete according to preferences and priorities.

Neither rule sees the realized preference shocks, landlord rankings or reference assignments. They use feasibility and saved recommendations.

There are two different matchings in this experiment. The anchor matching tells the platform which suggestions to add. It is a planning calculation. Then applicants try their favorite visible option. Each listing tentatively holds the highest-priority applicant it can accept. Anyone turned away tries the next apartment on their list. This is applicant-proposing deferred acceptance. The person the planner had in mind can still lose that apartment in the assignment. That gap is exactly where a retention question arises.

Both keep each person's list length fixed. But balancing can replace several options, while the anchor replaces at most one. Neither rule explicitly protects existing recipients.

SLIDE 9 — Does one coordinated suggestion help job seekers?
06:44–07:59 | 75 seconds

Consider the jobs replication. We use New York metro postings and survey-based seeker profiles. In each simulated market, people see up to five feasible jobs. The experiment pairs the same seekers and postings across policies. The outcome is the assignment in that simulation.

With GPT shortlists, 63.5 percent get a placement. Coordinate just one option on each list, and that rises to 79.4 percent. That is 612 additional placements on net across 3,840 seeker appearances. Then apply the same anchoring rule to random shortlists. That simple control reaches 81.4 percent. The improvement over plain GPT also shows up when the anchor starts with random suggestions. This comparison gives us no placement advantage to claim for the language model.

Here is what we can learn even with only the published totals. The anchor changed 920 personal lists. Under the fixed priorities in this jobs model, changing one list can create at most one new recipient. To see why, remove that seeker, then add them back. Rejections travel down a single chain. Several people can switch jobs, but only one person at a time is looking for a vacancy.

So there can be at most 920 entrants across these edits. Entrants minus exits equals the net gain of 612. Subtraction gives an upper bound of 308 exits, and zero is also consistent with the bounds. We cannot tell which individuals lost a match or measure coverage after the anchor from these totals. Chile's individual application records let us follow that question further.

SLIDE 10 — Who loses access in Chile?

In Chile we can follow individual records. We compare two classifiers trained on an earlier cohort to predict initial offers. The rank-informed policy gains 4,638 enrollment hits overall, but loses 1,670 placements compared with the rank-blind policy. Which families bear the loss?

For every hundred records in the study only about seven belong to this group. Yet about three quarters of the total net loss falls there.

In counts, that is 1,270 out of 1,670 net lost placements. We compare two simulated assignments for the same pupils. Actual school offers were never taken away. It also reminds us that matching historical enrollment isn't the same as improving welfare. To move beyond those averages, we need a precise requirement for who should remain protected.

SLIDE 12 — Choosing shortlists with zero exits is hard
09:36–10:37 | 61 seconds

We can write that requirement as a design problem.

Fix the baseline shortlists and a supplied set of priority draws. Choose one new policy that works across every draw. We want more reference hits, with every baseline recipient still getting a seat on each draw. They may switch schools, but they cannot become unplaced.

For the Chile application, we keep options shared by the rank-blind and rank-informed policies, choose the remaining options from their union, and preserve each list's length.

Deciding whether a target gain is achievable is NP-complete, even with unit seats and lists of at most three options. Checking a proposed policy with deferred acceptance is still polynomial.

The certificate is simply the proposed shortlists. We run deferred acceptance on every supplied draw, compare each recipient with the corresponding baseline, and count the hits. The shortlists stay fixed across draws; the resulting assignments can change. That is why verification is efficient, even though finding the best policy is hard.

This theorem uses fixed, strict priorities on an explicit panel. We test Chile's family-linked rules separately. The next picture explains where the computational difficulty comes from.

SLIDE 13 — Why individually safe edits can conflict
10:37–11:52 | 75 seconds

Start with a graph. Each vertex represents an edit that adds one reference hit. For each edge, we construct a priority draw where its two endpoint edits can interfere.

On this draw, choosing edit u displaces one already-matched pupil toward the fallback seat. That edit alone is safe.

Now choose edit v as well. A second pupil needs the same fallback seat, and one of them must lose a place.

Other vertex edits are harmless on this particular draw.

We repeat this construction for every edge. One fixed set of edits must work on every draw, so it cannot contain both endpoints of any edge. The safe choices form an independent set.

Each chosen edit adds one hit. Maximizing safe gains would therefore solve Independent Set.

The construction also includes a reference draw. Running full-list deferred acceptance there generates the reference assignments, so the reduction doesn't rely on arbitrary target labels. There is one conflict draw per graph edge, plus that one reference draw, so the instance grows polynomially. A safe gain of at least K exists exactly when K mutually nonadjacent vertices exist.

The same construction tells us something else. Imagine a graph made of disjoint edges. Keeping everyone's exact original assignment allows zero selected edits on the reference draw. Keeping everyone seated lets us choose one endpoint from each edge. With more edges, the gap can grow as large as we like. So an exact-assignment ceiling cannot automatically be used as a ceiling for seat retention.

SLIDE 14 — Allowing school switches leaves more room
11:52–13:02 | 70 seconds

Back in Chile, we compare two promises on the same design panels: 1,024 lotteries per cohort, for 2022 through 2024.

The stricter promise keeps every baseline recipient in the same course and seat type. Within our allowed shortlist family, the maximum additional enrollment coverage is exactly 314 hits.

The looser promise lets people switch schools, as long as everyone keeps a seat. We construct shortlists that gain 642 hits. The certified upper bound is 4,620.

Allowing switches gives us more than twice the demonstrated gain. But there's a wide gap to the upper bound. We haven't solved this problem to optimality.

The new shortlist must keep courses shared by the rank-blind and rank-informed policies. Its remaining slots come from the union of those two original lists. Its length stays fixed. That constraint makes the comparison interpretable. It also sets the scope of the number 314. A platform allowed to add arbitrary new courses would face a different optimization problem.

The gap measures what the optimization has left unresolved. We have a concrete policy for the lower endpoint and a mathematical bound for the upper endpoint. Protection covers every baseline recipient, including those without an enrollment link. Only the coverage objective needs that link.

These are hindsight benchmarks: they use the evaluation cohorts' later enrollment labels to choose edits. A real policy wouldn't have those labels before assignment. So we also test rules using only information available at that point.

SLIDE 15 — What happens on fresh lottery draws?
13:02–14:05 | 63 seconds

Here are two such rules. We freeze them after applications arrive and before the ordinary assignment. Neither uses that cohort's realized offers or later enrollment to select shortlists.

The structural rule restricts interactions around applicants without a guaranteed fallback. Its proof covers all permitted ordinary lottery orders, for the fixed inputs and reconstructed mechanism.

The proof starts with common options that already guarantee a pupil a seat. Around pupils lacking that guarantee, it freezes the relevant competitors and family interactions. This limits where edits can propagate. We keep the same three cohorts and draw fresh priorities. There are 384 course-level cohort draws and 96 cohort draws using school and family tickets. This tests new lottery orders for the same applicants.

It gains 28 enrollment hits and has zero exits in the test panels.

The learned rule passes its design-panel checks and gains 50 hits. But it has one exit in each of two fresh test panels.

The 28 are net additional historical-school hits across about one and a half million linked applicant-years. They count a school appearing on a list, not extra pupils getting seats. The practical gain is small. What matters here is the difference between a proof covering the allowed orders and a rule that happened to pass many simulated draws. More testing alone doesn't turn the second into the first.

SLIDE 16 — What happened when families received school advice?
14:05–14:53 | 48 seconds

Our replays held applications fixed. Another research team tested what real families did with advice. In the 2021 Chilean field experiment by Agte and colleagues, families were randomly assigned different versions of a school map. The basic version showed the schools. Another version gave counts by price and quality and highlighted inexpensive schools with medium or high official quality ratings. Families could apply anywhere.

Compared with the basic map, the highlighted version raised the share ranking a qualifying school second by 5.4 percentage points. But the estimate for receiving any assignment was only 0.2 points, with an approximate interval from minus two to plus 2.4 points. That leaves both gains and losses plausible.

The outcomes use different samples. A qualifying second choice met the criteria. We don't know whether that particular school appeared on that family's map. The trial establishes that families can respond to information. It doesn't tell us what would happen if everyone received our shortlists.

SLIDE 17 — Can a small trial get the direction wrong?
14:53–15:50 | 57 seconds

Here is a reason to care about that last distinction. Take a market with three pupils and three seats. The old rule shows the first two pupils school X, and the third pupil school Y. The new rule points everyone to Y. At Y, the second pupil has top priority, followed by the first and then the third.

Imagine that each pupil independently has a one-in-four chance of receiving the new rule. For the first pupil, switching hurts only when the second pupil also switches. For the second pupil, switching helps when the first pupil stays with X and takes that seat. The third pupil sees Y either way. That gives individual effects of minus one quarter, plus three quarters, and zero. Their average is positive, one sixth.

Now switch everyone. Under the old rule, X and Y each filled a seat. Under the new rule, everyone competes at Y. Only one seat fills. A positive direct effect in a small trial coexists with a drop from two placements to one at full rollout.

A field test of the whole policy should therefore track everyone sharing those seats, including families who never changed their own list.

SLIDE 18 — Protecting recipients can preserve exclusion
15:50–16:34 | 44 seconds

There's an ethical choice hidden in the word protect. Protect whom? Our structural rule guarantees a seat to every baseline recipient, yet across its paired test lotteries no previously unplaced pupil newly got a seat.

A district could honor that guarantee and leave the same families waiting. We'd ask for two numbers before adopting it. Of the children who would previously have received a seat, how many still do? And of those who previously went without, how many now receive one? These are separate obligations.

The school itself matters too. A child can keep a seat and end up at a school their family likes less. A historical enrollment match cannot tell us how that family values the change. Families should be able to add choices, correct a mistaken recommendation and ask for a human review. Those are safeguards we propose.

The housing example has the same limit. Coordination changes who competes for an affordable apartment. It cannot make an unaffordable apartment feasible.

SLIDE 19 — Evaluate the whole market
16:34–17:00 | 26 seconds

Go back to the apartment app. Those renters already saw every apartment they could qualify for. Their simulated match rate went from 13.1 to 27.5 percent because other people received different recommendations.

That is the evaluation we would take to a housing platform or school district. Follow everyone competing for the same places, including people whose own list stays fixed. Count who gets a place, who loses one, and who still waits. Thank you.
