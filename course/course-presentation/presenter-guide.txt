1. Opening
We study a matching market where clients cannot act on every feasible option.
A platform can pay to open options through their brokers. The question is
which brokers to audit when the platform keeps using the same matching rule.

2. The question
The original motivation is a broker steering a client toward a convenient
deal. We focus on access: some opportunities never reach the client.
We take that restriction as an input. We do not explain the broker's strategic
choice or estimate a commission effect.

Opening more options sounds helpful. The example will show why the response
of the matching algorithm still matters.

3. The market
B and S are buyers and sellers. Each has capacity one. Each client strictly
ranks the acceptable partners, with being unmatched last.

A firm is a fixed representative, possibly for several clients. A
side-exclusive firm serves only one side. The original four types of
participants therefore become two matched populations and their representatives.
For rentals, the sides could be renters and listing slots.

4. Stability in the full market
A pair blocks if both clients prefer each other to their current assignments.
The subscript E matters: we check every feasible pair, including hidden ones.

DA is stable on the graph it receives. Our question is whether its output
also survives a check against the full graph. The restricted-edge literature
already allows unavailable pairs to retain their blocking force.

5. Audits and deferred acceptance
Auditing either endpoint's firm opens a pair. One firm audit opens all feasible
pairs involving its clients. It enables a choice; it does not force a match.

An unmatched buyer proposes to its best available seller that has not rejected
it. Each seller holds its favorite proposal and rejects the rest. Rejected
buyers continue. The held proposals form the final matching.

We assume the planner knows the full graph and rankings. Audit costs pay for
making access usable, such as verifying eligibility or releasing a contact.
These are possible interpretations, not observations about an actual partner.

6. Two audit costs
Support asks whether some full-market stable matching is available.
Response asks whether DA actually chooses one. Every successful response is
a support witness, so the support cost is a lower bound.

Auditing all firms always succeeds. Running DA is polynomial-time.
The hard problem is choosing the cheapest set of audits, not running DA.

7. A small example
Let the audience read the rankings. With no audits, the only visible pairs
are ay, bx, and cz. This is full-market stable:
- a and c would prefer x, but x prefers its current partner b.
- b would prefer y, but y prefers its current partner a.

Auditing a and b gives them their first choices. Now x holds a, so x and c
both want to deviate. The pair cx is still hidden.

8. The rejection chain
Restart DA after auditing c. Let a and b propose first, giving ax and by.
Then c proposes to x and displaces a. The remaining chain is a to y,
b to x, and c back to z.

The original stable allocation returns. Neither preferences nor the full
market changed. Expanding the visible graph caused the algorithm to choose
a different outcome in the middle case. A successful audit set can lose
success when another audit is added.

9. Two audits versus N+2
Repeat the example N times. Firm F_A represents every a_i and F_B every b_i.
All other firms are private.

Each shared firm also has N+3 clients in isolated, initially hidden pairs.
Such a pair must be matched in any full-market stable matching: otherwise
both clients are unmatched and block. The shared audits open those pairs
cheaply, but they also create the unstable middle outcome in every copy.

The diagram shows the two common decisions and the N separate repairs.

10. Why the gap is exact
Support costs two. Auditing F_A and F_B opens every isolated pair and leaves
the original stable matching available in every copy. One audit cannot open
both isolated families.

A successful response costs at most N+2: audit both shared firms and each
c_i's firm. Omitting a shared firm costs at least N+4, because its N+3 private
seller substitutes are needed, plus one audit for the other family.
An optimum therefore uses both shared firms.

Every copy then needs c_i's or x_i's firm. Those repairs are disjoint, proving
the lower bound N+2. Appendix 1 gives the simpler singleton-firm version.
The cost separation does not by itself prove approximation hardness.

11. A two-by-two switch
This is a different example, used for the reduction. Initially p and q see x.
Seller x holds p, leaving q and y unmatched. The pair qy blocks.

Audit p's firm and the outcome becomes py, qx, both buyers' first choices.
Audit q's firm alone and the outcome becomes px, qy, also stable.
Audit y's firm and both hidden edges open. Audit x's firm and nothing changes.

Thus success is exactly an OR of three audit decisions. This particular
construction is monotone even though the general problem is not.

12. A reduction from Vertex Cover
A graph vertex becomes a buying firm. Each graph edge becomes one switch.
The graph degree equals the firm's number of clients, so maximum degree
three gives portfolios of at most three.

A vertex cover audits an endpoint of every switch and succeeds.
Conversely, remove useless x-firm audits and replace any private y-firm audit
by an endpoint buying firm. The positive OR condition preserves success,
and repeated replacements only reduce cost. We obtain a vertex cover no
larger than the audit plan.

The optimum costs coincide. This transfers NP-hardness and APX-hardness.
A proposed plan can be checked in polynomial time, so the budget decision
is NP-complete. We do not claim the general optimization problem is in APX.

13. The algorithm
The master chooses a plan that supports some full-market stable matching.
It gives a lower bound, but its proposed plan may fail when we run DA.

The DA check either verifies the plan or supplies a failure constraint.
The algorithm repeats. It can also stop when its lower bound reaches the
cost of an already verified plan. The implementation starts with reverse-delete.

The decomposition and stable-matching polytope are established methods.
Our constraints use the particular response that we need to verify.

14. The part of each list that matters
For x > y > z and a final match y, the full prefix contains x and y.
It includes x even if x was hidden. An unmatched buyer contributes its
whole acceptable list.

DA never proposes below its eventual partner. If another graph preserves
presence and absence throughout these prefixes, the same sequential
proposal run replays. The proof is induction on the tentative matching state.

A failed response therefore certifies that some prefix visibility must change.
Preserving only visible edges is not enough: a newly exposed better option
could change the run.

15. Constraints from a failed run
Whole-assignment cuts remove one firm-audit vector. Component cuts preserve
only decisions affecting a failed local market. Prefix cuts use the relevant
visible and hidden pairs.

For the example, visible ax and by with hidden cx force the failed response.
At (1,1,0), the displayed inequality has left side zero. A successful plan
must change that pattern. Changing it is necessary, not sufficient.

This is planning before implementation. We are not taking options away from
people after an allocation is committed.

16. What the solver certifies
Successful plans remain feasible for every master. Each failed run rules
out a new pattern. With exact master optimization, this process terminates.

The lower bound must be a valid master bound. The upper bound comes only
from a plan whose DA response passes the full-market check. A timeout returns
that verified plan and the remaining cost gap.

On the constructed family, prefix cuts need at most two master solves.
Whole-assignment cuts must separately reject at least 2^N-1 cheaper failures.
This compares specified methods. It is not a lower bound on all algorithms.

17. Synthetic markets
We use 24 clients per side and ten firms with unit costs. The eight scenario
cells cross component count, preference family, and mean initial exposure.
Each uses the same 20 seed blocks, giving 160 markets.

A partnership has probability 0.3 and multiplies exposure odds by four.
A randomly assigned half of buyers receive half the exposure odds.
That label is independent of preferences and does not identify a demographic
group. The intercept targets mean exposure of 0.25 or 0.50.

The feasible local markets are balanced complete blocks. This is a controlled
generator, not an estimate of housing geography.

18. Methods
At ten firms, exhaustive search checks only 1,024 plans, so it is a serious
baseline. All exact variants use the same support model and starting heuristic.

Reverse-delete begins with all firms and keeps a deletion only if DA passes,
repeating until no single deletion works. This is a local stopping rule.
Blocker greedy follows the best immediate blocker reduction per unit cost,
continuing even when an immediate reduction is unavailable.
Prefix cover exposes the full-DA prefixes. It is sufficient but can overpay.

19. Results on 160 markets
All exact variants agree with exhaustive search. Mean optimum is 4.7375 audits;
the seed-block 95% interval is [4.6625, 4.80625].

Reverse-delete reaches the minimum in 140 markets at a median of 1.4 ms.
That is a strong result for a simple baseline.

Prefix cuts have a slightly lower unpaired median than assignment cuts,
but their paired geometric runtime ratio is 1.007 with interval [0.995, 1.021].
The evidence does not establish a general speed advantage for prefix cuts.

20. The support lower bound
The ablation removes only the support model on 40 core markets: the first
five seeds in each scenario cell. At a five-second cap, certification falls
from 40 to 18. The median without support is at the cap, so do not interpret
the ratio of medians as an uncensored speedup.

In the full core, prefix cuts certify 151 markets after one master solve.
The first support candidate fails in 21 markets, but the support and response
optima differ in only eight. Another plan at the same cost can succeed.

21. Larger markets
Each case has 64 clients per side and 24 firms. All exact variants certify
9 of 16 connected and all 16 modular markets within fifteen seconds.

For prefix cuts, the largest unresolved gap is two cost units. Assignment
cuts have a gap as large as twelve. Every returned incumbent is verified.
A timeout does not establish that its incumbent is suboptimal.

Splitting markets changes list lengths as well as connectivity. A separate
32-case suite varies side-exclusive versus mixed portfolios and unit versus
integer costs. Prefix optimization matches exhaustive search in all 32.

22. Calls needed to certify the optimum
N counts copies of the three-by-three market from slides 9-10. One master
call solves the current audit optimization problem. The table reports how
many calls each method needed to certify an optimum, under a ten-second cap.

Read across a row to compare methods on the same instance. For N=2,
assignment cuts need 131 calls, component cuts four, and prefix cuts two.
For every tested N from three onward, assignment cuts time out.
Prefix certifies all eight instances in two calls. Component takes three
to seven. This is a deliberately constructed family, not a random sample.

Timeout means no optimality certificate before the cap, not that the method
found no feasible plan. It still returns a verified plan. A timeout gives
no measurement of the total calls that finishing would require.

For N=10, prefix certifies cost 12. Assignment returns a verified cost-26
plan after 169 recorded calls, including its final interrupted call.
Those 169 calls are not a completed-run result and are not plotted in the
table. The raw counts remain in data/results.json.

The theoretical comparison is on slide 16 and Appendix 5:
at least 2^N-1 failed whole-assignment calls versus two prefix master solves.
It is a lower bound for the specified cut method, not a fitted curve.

23. Who gains, and who loses?
Rank score is zero when unmatched and otherwise 1-(rank-1)/list length.
This is an ordinal summary, not a cardinal measure of welfare.

For the prefix solver's selected optimal plans, lower-access buyers gain
0.280 in mean rank score, but 14.4% receive a worse-ranked partner.
The corresponding higher-access figures are 0.162 and 18.9%.
Both panels have 95% seed-block bootstrap intervals.

The baseline is the provisional DA allocation before audits. The experiment
does not model revoking an existing lease.

24. Different optimal plans
We replayed all 614 minimum-cost plans. Audit sets differ in 148 markets,
and the induced allocations differ in 37.

Every cheapest plan worsens at least one buyer in 155 markets, at least one
seller in 159, and at least one lower-access buyer in 135. The affected client
need not be the same across plans.

The quantifier is over minimum-cost plans. The result does not rule out a
more expensive stable intervention that avoids losses.

25. Housing and AI recommendations
An AI recommender is one possible source of the initial exposure graph.
Our current experiments generate that graph synthetically.

For an application, we need a service that knows the reference catalogue,
can enable access through groups of clients, and retains DA as its matching
rule. Decentralized housing sales do not automatically satisfy these assumptions.

Liu et al. motivate examining housing recommendation exposure. Their work
does not validate our audit model. The listing study in the appendix remains
a proposal. We have not measured a real AI system or a housing effect.

26. Related work
Unavailable blockers, costly interviews, preference discovery, and matching
interventions have substantial precedents.

Trabelsi et al. are particularly close: a facilitator pays to add pairs while
a platform retains its allocation rule. They study maximum matching and
participation guarantees. We use grouped access decisions, a fixed DA response,
and full-market stability. We impose no no-harm condition in the current model.

The candidate contribution is this precise problem and its analysis.
Neither the ingredients nor the proof techniques should be presented as new
in isolation. Establishing publication novelty requires further review.

27. Conclusions
The distinction is between making a stable allocation possible and making
the platform's rule choose it. That distinction changes the cost.

We give structural examples, a hardness reduction, and an exact method with
verifiable bounds. The experiments also show when simpler methods compete
and why stability and cost alone do not settle the social objective.

The remaining application question is whether a real institution fits the
access and matching assumptions.

COMMON QUESTIONS

Why preserve DA?
The model assumes that the institution retains this rule. If the planner
can directly choose the allocation, support is the appropriate objective.
A real application needs to justify the fixed-rule assumption.

Why does this not contradict DA's buyer optimality?
Buyer optimality compares stable matchings on a fixed input graph. Our
interventions change that graph. A new output can be stable on the visible
graph while blocked by a hidden pair in the full graph.

Is the support problem polynomial?
For a fixed audit set, support feasibility is a restricted-edge stable-matching
problem. Optimizing the grouped audit choices still involves binary decisions.
Continuous matching variables do not make the whole problem polynomial.

Why not audit a blocker's endpoints?
Auditing another firm can change the assignment and remove the incentive to
block. In the switch, auditing p's firm fixes qy. Restricting the search to
the blocker endpoints can therefore miss a cheaper solution.

Why are all final core allocations perfect?
Each feasible component is balanced and complete. An unmatched buyer and
seller would block. Perfectness follows from the generator, not from solving
a housing shortage.

Does the binary partnership result guarantee a solution?
No. It gives a polynomial-time existence test via partial-order super-stability.
The two profiles may have different unique stable matchings. Three partnership
levels break this reduction, which alone proves no hardness result.

What exactly is proposed in the listing study?
Hold the catalogue and recommendation count fixed, randomize listing order,
repeat prompts, and record valid listing IDs. Use Census household data only
to calibrate fictional profiles. Both-side rankings and firm identities would
need justification or sensitivity analysis. Inquiry-interest labels are not
individual preference lists.

The access-cost extension adds an average affordable-option exposure target.
Households with no affordable feasible options must be reported separately.
This extension has not been evaluated.
